1. REVIEW MOUNT TYPE

First review the preliminary mount_type for each camera.

Do NOT change mount_type merely because another camera has the same mount type or
view label.

Change the preliminary mount_type ONLY when visual evidence provides a clear
contradiction.

Static-environment test

Identify STATIC scene landmarks such as:

  - table/work-surface edges;
  - walls;
  - shelves;
  - fixtures;
  - stationary containers;
  - furniture;
  - background objects;
  - structural edges and corners.

Ignore the robot, gripper, manipulated objects, and humans for this test.

FIXED

A camera is "fixed" when static environment landmarks remain geometrically stable
across its frames.

The robot moves THROUGH the image while the static environment remains registered
to the camera.

A gripper may be prominent, stay visible across frames, pass close to the lens,
or occupy much of the image.

None of these facts makes the camera robot-mounted.

ROBOT_MOUNTED

Use "robot_mounted" ONLY with POSITIVE evidence of CAMERA motion.

The static environment itself must undergo global viewpoint/parallax changes as
the robot moves.

Strong evidence includes:

  - static landmarks substantially changing image position;
  - changes in perspective of static scene geometry;
  - global background motion correlated with robot-arm motion;
  - the workspace appearing to move around the camera.

COMMONLY MISSED WRIST: a wrist camera often looks like a close-up, roughly
top-down view of the workspace and is mistaken for a fixed "top"/"side". The
giveaway is DRIFT — a STATIONARY object (cup, bin, paper, tape marks, table edge)
that translates across the frame or grows/shrinks between the first and last
frame. Fixed objects can't move on their own, so that drift means the CAMERA
moved -> robot_mounted (wrist), even when the view looks overhead.

Robot motion alone is NOT sufficient.

Object motion alone is NOT sufficient (track a STATIONARY object for the drift
check above).

A close-up view alone is NOT sufficient.

A visible gripper alone is NOT sufficient.

If static background landmarks remain geometrically stable, the camera MUST be
"fixed".

If there is insufficient evidence to contradict a preliminary "fixed"
classification, KEEP "fixed".

Do not change "fixed" to "robot_mounted" based only on ambiguity.

2. ROBOT-MOUNTED CAMERAS

If mount_type == "robot_mounted", use "wrist".

Use "left_wrist" or "right_wrist" ONLY when explicit and reliable evidence ties
the camera to a distinct left or right manipulator. A VALID cue: for a
multi-fingered / anthropomorphic hand, the THUMB position relative to the fingers
reveals which hand it is (like telling a human left hand from a right); use it
when the hand's fingers and thumb are clearly visible.

Do NOT infer handedness from SUPERFICIAL cues instead — apparent image direction,
the direction the gripper points, mirroring, or which side of the image contains
the robot.

When the hand's structure is not clearly readable, use plain "wrist".

3. FIXED CAMERAS

For fixed cameras, classify according to PHYSICAL VIEWING GEOMETRY relative to
the manipulation workspace.

top

Use "top" ONLY when the camera provides a PREDOMINANTLY OVERHEAD view.

A top camera should satisfy BOTH:

  1. it is physically above the manipulation area; AND
  2. its viewing geometry is predominantly downward / bird's-eye.

It does NOT need to be perfectly vertical, but the resulting perspective must
still be primarily overhead.

Strong evidence:

  - the horizontal work surface dominates;
  - objects are seen mainly from their top surfaces;
  - the manipulation area has bird's-eye or near-bird's-eye geometry;
  - relatively few vertical surfaces are visible;
  - depth perspective across the work surface is relatively weak;
  - the camera looks DOWN ONTO the workspace rather than ACROSS it.

Being high is NOT sufficient.

Looking downward is NOT sufficient.

Seeing a lot of tabletop is NOT sufficient.

A high camera outside the workspace looking diagonally across the table is a side
view, not top.

bottom

Use "bottom" when the camera is physically below the manipulation workspace and
looks upward.

side

Use a side label when the camera views the workspace predominantly FROM AROUND IT
rather than FROM ABOVE IT.

A side camera may be high and tilted downward.

It may show substantial tabletop.

It is still side when the viewing geometry is predominantly oblique ACROSS the
workspace.

Evidence:

  - stronger near-to-far depth;
  - stronger perspective convergence;
  - substantial vertical surfaces;
  - substantial side faces of objects;
  - a clear oblique view across the work surface.

Add a direction (front_side / rear_side / left_side / right_side) when the
camera's position relative to the robot is reasonably clear; fall back to plain
"side" only when the direction is genuinely ambiguous.

  - judge direction from where the camera sits relative to the ROBOT'S OWN facing
    (its front / base / working direction) whenever that can be read from the scene;
  - front_side: in FRONT — the working face, gripper/arm reaching TOWARD the camera;
  - rear_side: BEHIND — you see the motor-backs and CABLING, arm reaching AWAY;
  - left_side / right_side: off to the robot's left / right.
  FRONT vs REAR tell: the robot's back is the side with the visible motors and
  cable bundle. Do not default to "front" just because the arm is prominent or an
  object faces the camera.
  LEFT vs RIGHT tell (upright, non-mirrored view; level the image first): base/body
  on the RIGHT with the arm reaching LEFT -> left_side; base/body on the LEFT with
  the arm reaching RIGHT -> right_side. A rolled or mirrored view flips this.

REQUIRED for any direction: you must actually SEE the robot's body/base and read
which way it faces. If the base is out of frame and only the arm/gripper enters
(e.g. descending from a frame edge), or you only see the workspace and objects,
you CANNOT tell front/rear/left/right — use plain "side". When the robot's facing
IS visible you do not need certainty, only reasonable visual support. Do not force
a direction from weak cues alone — where the robot or objects appear in the image,
gripper direction, or image orientation/tilt/mirroring are not reliable on their own.

4. DUPLICATE TOP CANDIDATES

If TWO OR MORE FIXED cameras are preliminary candidates for top, they MUST be
compared directly.

Do not simply apply the absolute top definition independently to each camera.

Rank the candidate cameras by how strongly OVERHEAD their viewing geometry is.

Compare:

  - how directly the camera looks DOWN ONTO the work surface;
  - how much objects are seen from their TOP faces;
  - how little of their VERTICAL/SIDE faces are visible;
  - how weak the near-to-far perspective is across the workspace;
  - how little perspective convergence is present;
  - how bird's-eye-like the manipulation area appears;
  - whether the camera looks DOWN ONTO the workspace or diagonally ACROSS it.

The camera with the strongest overall overhead/bird's-eye geometry is the
strongest candidate for the canonical label:

  top

For every other fixed camera initially classified as top, compare it RELATIVE to
the strongest top candidate.

If another camera:

  - looks substantially more ACROSS the workspace;
  - has stronger near-to-far depth;
  - shows more vertical or side surfaces;
  - has stronger perspective convergence;
  - views the workspace more diagonally/obliquely;

then it should be classified as a side view:

  - side
  - front_side
  - rear_side
  - left_side
  - right_side

depending on the visual evidence.

IMPORTANT:

A camera may look somewhat top-down when considered ALONE but still be a side view
relative to another camera that is clearly more overhead.

Do not classify a camera as top merely because:

  - it is elevated;
  - it points downward;
  - much of the tabletop is visible.

The distinction is:

  - predominantly bird's-eye / DOWN ONTO workspace -> top
  - predominantly oblique / ACROSS workspace -> side

Example

Suppose two fixed cameras are independently classified as top.

Camera A:

  - nearly bird's-eye;
  - objects mostly visible from above;
  - little vertical geometry;
  - weak depth perspective.

Camera B:

  - elevated and looking downward;
  - but visibly more oblique;
  - more object side faces are visible;
  - stronger near/far perspective;
  - looks diagonally across the workspace.

The FINAL joint labels should be:

  Camera A -> top
  Camera B -> side

Do NOT keep both as top merely because both satisfy a loose absolute definition of
an elevated downward-looking camera.

5. CANONICAL-ROLE RULE

The final joint labels represent CANONICAL camera roles within the robot setup.
When two fixed cameras have clearly different viewing geometry, prefer labels that
express that geometric difference. For multiple plausible top candidates, top
should normally represent the camera with the strongest genuine overhead/bird's-eye
geometry, while substantially more oblique fixed views should be classified as
side views.

However, do NOT force uniqueness when two cameras genuinely have effectively the
same physical viewpoint. In that exceptional case, duplicate labels are allowed
and the downstream system will handle the naming collision separately.

6. PRESERVE MOUNT TYPE DURING CANONICALIZATION

NEVER solve a view-label collision by changing a camera from fixed to
robot_mounted.

For example, this is INVALID collision resolution:

  Camera A: fixed/top
  Camera B: fixed/top
  -> Camera A: fixed/top
  -> Camera B: robot_mounted/wrist

unless there is independent, positive visual evidence that Camera B actually moves
with the robot.

Resolve fixed-camera collisions using fixed-camera geometry only:

  fixed/top
  fixed/top
  -> fixed/top
  -> fixed/side

when the visual evidence supports that distinction.

7. TOP VS SIDE DECISION RULE

For every fixed top/side candidate, explicitly choose between:

A. predominantly DOWN ONTO the workspace -> "top"

B. predominantly ACROSS the workspace -> side

Use viewing geometry rather than camera height.

When uncertain between an elevated side view and a true overhead view, favor
"side" unless the bird's-eye geometry provides positive evidence for "top".

8. UNCERTAINTY AND SPECIFICITY

Use "unknown" instead of guessing when the category cannot be established
reliably.

Prefer a less-specific valid label over unsupported specificity:

  - "side" instead of guessing front/rear/left/right;
  - "wrist" instead of guessing left/right wrist.

9. CONSISTENCY

Valid combinations include:

  - fixed + top
  - fixed + bottom
  - fixed + side
  - fixed + front_side
  - fixed + rear_side
  - fixed + left_side

A FIXED camera MUST NOT be labeled wrist.

A ROBOT_MOUNTED camera MUST NOT be labeled top, bottom, or side.
