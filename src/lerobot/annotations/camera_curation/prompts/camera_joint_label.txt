STEP 1 — MOUNT TYPE

For EACH camera, first determine:

  - "fixed"
  - "robot_mounted"
  - "unknown"

A robot-mounted camera is physically attached to the moving arm, wrist, gripper,
or end effector.

Determine this from TEMPORAL behavior.

ROBOT_MOUNTED:

  - the whole viewpoint moves with robot motion;
  - the background/workspace undergoes egocentric viewpoint/parallax changes;
  - a visible wrist/gripper may stay relatively stable in camera coordinates;
  - the workspace appears to move around the camera.

FIXED:

  - static scene geometry stays in approximately the same camera coordinate
    system;
  - the robot moves through the image;
  - the arm/gripper may move substantially;
  - the camera viewpoint itself remains geometrically stationary.

The presence or prominence of a gripper is NOT evidence of a wrist camera.

If the background remains fixed while the robot moves through the image, the
camera MUST be "fixed", not "robot_mounted".

STEP 2 — ROBOT-MOUNTED CAMERAS

If a camera is "robot_mounted", classify it as:

  - "wrist"

Use "left_wrist" or "right_wrist" ONLY when explicit and reliable evidence ties
the camera to a distinct left or right manipulator.

Do not infer handedness from apparent image direction, gripper orientation,
mirroring, or the side of the image containing the arm.

Otherwise use plain "wrist".

STEP 3 — FIXED CAMERAS

For each fixed camera, determine its PHYSICAL POSITION relative to the
manipulation workspace.

top

Use "top" when the camera is positioned predominantly ABOVE the manipulation
workspace and looks predominantly downward.

It does NOT need to be perfectly vertical.

An elevated oblique camera is still "top" when its physical location is primarily
above the workspace.

Evidence can include:

  - strong visibility of horizontal work surfaces;
  - objects seen substantially from above;
  - a global/overhead view of the manipulation area;
  - relatively compressed perspective along the work surface.

bottom

Use "bottom" when the camera is physically below the manipulation workspace and
looks upward.

side

A camera positioned predominantly OUTSIDE the workspace boundary and looking
INWARD is a side view.

It remains a side view even if it is elevated and looks downward.

Compared with an overhead view, side views generally show stronger depth
perspective and more vertical surfaces.

When reliably identifiable, use:

  - "front_side"
  - "rear_side"
  - "left_side"
  - "right_side"

Otherwise use plain "side".

Do NOT infer direction merely from where the robot or objects appear within the
image.

STEP 4 — COMPARE FIXED CAMERAS

Use the cameras relative to one another as additional geometric evidence.

When distinguishing elevated fixed cameras:

  - the camera whose physical viewpoint is predominantly ABOVE the manipulation
    area is a stronger candidate for "top";
  - a camera viewing the same workspace primarily from OUTSIDE one of its
    boundaries is a side view, even if elevated;
  - compare how much horizontal work surface versus vertical scene geometry each
    camera exposes;
  - compare perspective depth and object foreshortening;
  - mentally account for camera roll, tilt, rotation, and possible mirroring.

Do NOT assume there must be exactly one top camera.

Do NOT assume there must be exactly one side camera.

Do NOT assign different labels merely because the cameras are different physical
devices.

Multiple cameras may share a label when the visual evidence supports the same
semantic viewpoint.

STEP 5 — UNCERTAINTY

Use "unknown" instead of guessing when the available visual evidence cannot
reliably establish the category.

Prefer a less-specific valid label over an unsupported specific one:

  - use "side" instead of guessing left/right/front/rear;
  - use "wrist" instead of guessing left/right wrist.
