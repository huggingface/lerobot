1. REVIEW MOUNT TYPE

First review the preliminary mount_type for each camera.

Do NOT change mount_type merely because another camera has the same mount type or
view label.

Change the preliminary mount_type ONLY when visual evidence provides a clear
contradiction.

Static-environment test

Identify STATIC scene landmarks such as:

  - table/work-surface edges;
  - walls;
  - shelves;
  - fixtures;
  - stationary containers;
  - furniture;
  - background objects;
  - structural edges and corners.

Ignore the robot, gripper, manipulated objects, and humans for this test.

FIXED

A camera is "fixed" when static environment landmarks remain geometrically stable
across its frames.

The robot moves THROUGH the image while the static environment remains registered
to the camera.

A gripper that MOVES THROUGH the frame — entering, crossing, and exiting while the
background stays put — does not make the camera robot-mounted; that is a fixed
camera watching the arm pass by. (Contrast: a gripper ANCHORED in the foreground
that moves WITH the camera is a wrist — see below.)

ROBOT_MOUNTED

Use "robot_mounted" ONLY with POSITIVE evidence of CAMERA motion.

The static environment itself must undergo global viewpoint/parallax changes as
the robot moves.

Strong evidence includes:

  - static landmarks substantially changing image position;
  - changes in perspective of static scene geometry;
  - global background motion correlated with robot-arm motion;
  - the workspace appearing to move around the camera.

COMMONLY MISSED WRIST: a wrist camera often looks like a close-up, roughly
top-down view of the workspace and is mistaken for a fixed "top"/"side". The
giveaway is DRIFT — a STATIONARY object (cup, bin, paper, tape marks, table edge)
that translates across the frame or grows/shrinks between the first and last
frame. Fixed objects can't move on their own, so that drift means the CAMERA
moved -> robot_mounted (wrist), even when the view looks overhead. Also: if the
WHOLE composition re-frames each shot (the same box/container at a different
position/scale/angle), the camera is moving -> wrist. BEWARE a blank backdrop
(green screen, plain wall) — it has no trackable texture and can look "static"
while the camera swings; track the concrete OBJECTS, not the blank background, and
do not infer fixed from an unchanging blank backdrop.

A GRIPPER FIXED IN THE FOREGROUND, ALIGNED WITH THE VIEW (decisive wrist cue): the
wrist signature is the robot's own gripper parked in the NEAR FOREGROUND, pointing
FORWARD along the camera's viewing direction and staying in the SAME place /
orientation in EVERY frame — as if camera and gripper are bolted together (Pi-robot:
dark jaws at the bottom edge reaching up into the scene, frame after frame). This
is decisive on its own: the gripper need NOT open (it may stay closed) and the
background need NOT drift. What matters is a gripper LOCKED to the camera in the
foreground. Merely SEEING a gripper is NOT it: a fixed camera sees the gripper
TRAVEL through the frame at changing positions/angles as the arm moves, an overhead
sees it reach DOWN onto the workspace, and a base / bracket / arm-link in a corner
over a static background is a fixed camera bolted next to the robot. So: gripper
parked in the foreground aligned with the view, same place every frame -> wrist;
otherwise not decisive, fall back to the background-drift test.

Robot motion alone is NOT sufficient.

A LARGE ARM SWINGING IN AND OUT IS FIXED, NOT WRIST. A big arm that sweeps across
the frame or enters and leaves it (common on an overhead/top view — it swoops in
to pick, then retracts) is the ARM moving through a stationary scene, however much
of the frame it covers. Do NOT read dramatic ARM motion as CAMERA motion. The
decider is the STATIC BACKGROUND: if the table / baseplate grid / corner markers /
workspace boundary stay REGISTERED across frames, it is FIXED no matter how
violently the arm moves; only the static landmarks themselves drifting means wrist.

Object motion alone is NOT sufficient (track a STATIONARY object for the drift
check above).

A close-up alone is not decisive — but a gripper anchored in the foreground leans
wrist (per the cue above), while a gripper crossing a stable scene is fixed.

A visible gripper alone is NOT sufficient.

If static background landmarks remain geometrically stable, the camera MUST be
"fixed".

If there is insufficient evidence to contradict a preliminary "fixed"
classification, KEEP "fixed".

Do not change "fixed" to "robot_mounted" based only on ambiguity.

2. ROBOT-MOUNTED CAMERAS

If mount_type == "robot_mounted", use "wrist".

When TWO cameras are robot_mounted, they are the robot's TWO HANDS — a LEFT/RIGHT
MIRROR PAIR: one is the left hand, one is the right. Do NOT leave both as plain
"wrist"; actively differentiate them from the HAND SHAPE. For a multi-fingered /
anthropomorphic hand, a left and a right hand are mirror images, so the THUMB sits
on opposite sides of the fingers: read each hand's palm/back and thumb position
and assign "left_wrist" / "right_wrist" accordingly. Compare the two hands against
each other — they should come out as opposite hands.

Fall back to plain "wrist" only for a simple 2-jaw parallel gripper (no thumb) or
when the hands genuinely cannot be read. Do NOT infer handedness from SUPERFICIAL
cues — use the hand's OWN anatomy, never apparent image direction, the direction
the gripper points, mirroring, or which side of the image contains the robot.

When the hand's structure is not clearly readable, use plain "wrist".

3. FIXED CAMERAS

For fixed cameras, classify according to PHYSICAL VIEWING GEOMETRY relative to
the manipulation workspace. Use the preliminary label as evidence, but revise it
when direct comparison provides stronger geometric evidence.

top

Use "top" for a genuinely OVERHEAD or NEAR-OVERHEAD camera.

The camera should be physically above the manipulation area and look mainly
DOWN ONTO the workspace. This is a canonical overhead role, but it does NOT need
to be perfect nadir / bird's-eye.

Positive evidence for "top":

  - the camera is physically above / over the workspace;
  - the manipulation area is primarily seen from above;
  - the work surface is the dominant scene plane;
  - object TOP faces are prominent;
  - the camera optical axis points mainly downward toward the manipulation region;
  - the overall scene reads as overhead or near-overhead, even if some perspective
    remains.

IMPORTANT: the following do NOT disqualify a true top camera by themselves:

  - moderate perspective;
  - some visible object side faces;
  - some visible robot vertical structure;
  - a small amount of wall/background;
  - a camera that is slightly offset from the exact workspace center.

Use "side" instead when the camera is clearly positioned around the workspace
perimeter and views it diagonally / obliquely from the side.

Practical rule:

  - physically over workspace + mainly downward -> top;
  - physically beside/perimeter + diagonal across workspace -> side.

When uncertain, do NOT decide from a single cue. Combine camera position,
viewing direction, dominant planes, and perspective.

bottom

Use "bottom" when the camera is physically below the manipulation workspace and
looks upward.

side

Use a side label for ANY camera physically positioned around / beside the
workspace rather than directly overhead. This includes elevated cameras looking
strongly downward from the workspace perimeter.

A side camera may be elevated and tilted downward. The deciding factor is the physical camera role: cameras positioned around or
beside the workspace are side cameras, including elevated cameras tilted strongly
downward.

Evidence supporting side includes stronger near/far depth, perspective
convergence, substantial side/vertical surfaces, and an obvious oblique view
across the workspace. These are supporting cues, not independent hard rules.

TOP VS SIDE — PRIMARY GEOMETRIC TEST

For every fixed top/side candidate compare:

  A. genuinely OVERHEAD / near-nadir -> top
  B. any clearly OBLIQUE / diagonal / perimeter view -> side

Use physical camera position relative to the workspace as the PRIMARY criterion,
with viewing direction as supporting evidence.

A visible wall, visible robot column, or visible vertical surfaces do NOT
automatically mean side. Likewise, an elevated camera or a large visible
tabletop do NOT automatically mean top.

When borderline, ask whether the camera is physically OVER the workspace or
around its PERIMETER. If that cannot be established, prefer side. Reserve top
for a clearly genuine overhead camera role.

SIDE DIRECTION — CAMERA POSITION IN THE ROBOT FRAME

Once a camera is classified as side, directional labels describe the CAMERA'S
PHYSICAL POSITION relative to the ROBOT'S OWN coordinate frame:

  - front_side: camera physically in front of the robot;
  - rear_side: camera physically behind the robot;
  - left_side: camera physically on the robot's left;
  - right_side: camera physically on the robot's right.

First establish the robot's physical front/back orientation. The front is its
working direction: where it reaches and where the active manipulation workspace
lies. Motor backs, mounting structure, and cabling can support identification of
the back, but object/workspace geometry is generally stronger evidence.

Do NOT derive left/right from image-space direction. In particular, do NOT use:

  - front/workspace points image-left -> left_side;
  - front/workspace points image-right -> right_side;
  - robot body appears on one side of the image;
  - arm enters from one image edge.

Image-space relationships can flip under roll, mirroring, and camera pose.

Assign left_side or right_side ONLY when visible 3D geometry, a known asymmetric
robot feature, or DIRECT MULTI-CAMERA COMPARISON lets you establish the robot's
physical left/right orientation and therefore the camera's physical side.

The joint view can provide NEW evidence. For example, two cameras may reveal
complementary robot geometry that makes their physical positions clear. Use that
evidence when it is genuinely present.

However, do NOT assign opposite left/right labels merely because two cameras
look different, because they collide, or because there are two side cameras.

If the camera is clearly lateral/oblique but its physical direction cannot be
established reliably, use plain "side". Plain side is a valid canonical role and
is preferable to unsupported specificity.


STRICT JOINT TOP RULE


TOP REQUIRES POSITIVE OVERHEAD EVIDENCE

Do not infer "top" merely because side evidence is weak. However, positive
overhead evidence does NOT require a perfect bird's-eye view.

A camera can still be "top" when it is slightly offset, angled, or shows some
vertical structure, as long as it is physically above the workspace and mainly
looks down onto the manipulation region.


The joint pass MUST NOT turn an oblique/elevated side camera into "top" merely
because it is the most overhead-looking camera among the available streams.

"top" is an ABSOLUTE geometric role, not a relative winner.

If none of the fixed cameras is genuinely overhead / near-overhead and physically
over the workspace, it is valid for the episode to have NO "top" camera.
Conversely, if one camera clearly has that overhead role, preserve it as "top"
even when it shows moderate perspective or some vertical structure.

Likewise, multiple side cameras are expected. Do not force one of them to become
top for canonical uniqueness.



JOINT LEFT / RIGHT INFERENCE FROM ROBOT LAYOUT

For fixed cameras already classified as SIDE views, actively use the robot's
layout to resolve left_side versus right_side.

Strong heuristic:

  - robot body/base on image RIGHT + arm reaching LEFT -> left_side;
  - robot body/base on image LEFT  + arm reaching RIGHT -> right_side.

When two side cameras form complementary views of the same robot/workspace, the
pair may provide extra evidence for left/right direction. Use that evidence only
when the body/reach geometry is clear. Do not force opposite labels merely because
there are two cameras.

This is a ROBOT-GEOMETRY heuristic, not a raw image-coordinate rule: consider
both body/base location and reach direction. If mirroring is suspected or the
robot geometry is too ambiguous, fall back to plain side.



JOINT DIRECTIONAL RESOLUTION — DO NOT LEAVE CLEAR PAIRS AS PLAIN SIDE

For each fixed camera already identified as SIDE, explicitly attempt directional
resolution before returning "side".

Apply:

  robot body/base on image RIGHT + arm reaching LEFT -> left_side
  robot body/base on image LEFT  + arm reaching RIGHT -> right_side

Judge EACH camera's direction from its OWN body/reach geometry, INDEPENDENTLY. Two
side cameras are NOT necessarily a left/right pair:

  - DIFFERENT layouts may resolve to opposite sides:
      camera A: robot on image RIGHT, reaching LEFT -> left_side
      camera B: robot on image LEFT,  reaching RIGHT -> right_side
  - the SAME layout gets the SAME label — a legitimate duplicate, NOT a problem:
      camera A: robot on image RIGHT, reaching LEFT -> left_side
      camera B: robot on image RIGHT, reaching LEFT -> left_side   (BOTH left_side)

NEVER flip a camera whose OWN geometry shows robot-on-image-RIGHT + reaching-LEFT
to right_side just to differentiate it from another left_side camera, or to make a
"complementary pair". Two cameras on the same side of the robot are a valid
duplicate; the downstream system numbers them (left_side_1, left_side_2).

Duplicate or plain "side" labels remain valid when the directional evidence is not
strong enough.

Plain "side" is a fallback only when the body/reach geometry is genuinely
ambiguous, contradictory, or likely mirrored.

The joint comparison is especially useful here: a complementary pair provides
additional confidence that the two cameras occupy opposite lateral sides of the
robot/workspace.


PAIRED LATERAL CAMERAS — IMPORTANT

When two FIXED cameras observe the same robot/workspace from opposite or
substantially different lateral directions, treat that pair as strong evidence
that BOTH cameras are SIDE views.

Do NOT label one camera "top" merely because one member of the pair is mounted
higher, points downward more strongly, or shows more tabletop.

Before assigning "top" to any fixed camera, compare it against the other fixed
views and ask:

  1. Is this camera actually physically OVER the workspace?
  2. Or is it another perimeter / corner / side camera looking diagonally down?

If the camera has the visual character of one member of a left/right lateral
pair, it should remain a side view UNLESS there is clear independent evidence
that it is actually physically overhead.

In particular, if two cameras show complementary / opposite views of the robot
body or workspace from around the table, classify both as SIDE first. Then, only
if the robot coordinate frame can be established, refine them to left_side and
right_side.

Correct pattern:

  lateral camera A -> side / left_side / right_side
  lateral camera B -> side / left_side / right_side

Incorrect pattern:

  lateral camera A -> side
  lateral camera B -> top

unless camera B is genuinely positioned over the workspace and has clear
near-bird's-eye geometry independent of camera A.

4. MULTIPLE TOP CANDIDATES

If TWO OR MORE FIXED cameras are preliminary candidates for "top", evaluate EACH
camera against the absolute top-vs-side geometry.

Do NOT rank them in order to select a single canonical "top".

Do NOT demote one camera merely because another camera is more overhead.

For each camera independently:

  - physically over workspace + mainly looking down -> top;
  - physically around/beside workspace + diagonal/oblique across it -> side.

Cross-camera comparison may help clarify ambiguous geometry, but the final label
for one camera must not depend on whether another camera already received the
same label.

Valid outcomes include:

  Camera A -> top
  Camera B -> top

when both are genuinely overhead.

Also valid:

  Camera A -> side
  Camera B -> side

when both are genuinely lateral.

And valid:

  Camera A -> top
  Camera B -> side

when their actual physical geometry differs.

There is NO preferred number of cameras per label.

5. LABEL-IDENTITY RULE

Final labels describe each camera's physical role, independently of how many
other cameras share that role.

Never change a label for the purpose of:

  - making labels unique;
  - increasing diversity;
  - avoiding a naming collision;
  - assigning each camera a different semantic role;
  - ensuring one camera fills each available category.

If two cameras are physically equivalent or sufficiently similar for this
taxonomy, KEEP THE SAME LABEL.

Only assign different labels when the visual evidence itself supports a
meaningful geometric difference.

6. PRESERVE MOUNT TYPE DURING CANONICALIZATION

NEVER solve a view-label collision by changing a camera from fixed to
robot_mounted.

For example, this is INVALID collision resolution:

  Camera A: fixed/top
  Camera B: fixed/top
  -> Camera A: fixed/top
  -> Camera B: robot_mounted/wrist

unless there is independent, positive visual evidence that Camera B actually moves
with the robot.

If two fixed cameras share the same label, that is not a collision that must be
resolved. Keep both labels unless the visual evidence independently supports a
different classification for one of them.

7. TOP VS SIDE DECISION RULE

For every fixed top/side candidate, explicitly choose between:

A. predominantly DOWN ONTO the workspace -> "top"

B. predominantly ACROSS the workspace -> side

Use viewing geometry rather than camera height.

When genuinely uncertain between an elevated across-the-workspace view and a
looking-down overhead view, lean "side" — but a clear angled-down overhead view is
"top" even with moderate perspective; do not demote it just for lacking a perfect
straight-down bird's-eye.

8. UNCERTAINTY AND SPECIFICITY

Use "unknown" instead of guessing when the category cannot be established
reliably.

Prefer a less-specific valid label over unsupported specificity:

  - "side" instead of guessing front/rear/left/right;
  - "wrist" instead of guessing left/right wrist.


GLOBAL ANTI-DIVERSITY RULE

The set of final labels does NOT need to be diverse, unique, balanced, or
one-per-role.

Never ask "which camera should get this label?"
Ask only "what label does THIS camera's geometry support?"

A result such as:

  Camera 1 -> side
  Camera 2 -> side
  Camera 3 -> side

is fully valid if all three cameras are genuinely side views.

Likewise:

  Camera 1 -> top
  Camera 2 -> top

is fully valid if both cameras are genuinely overhead.

Duplicate final labels are expected and must be preserved whenever the physical
views are duplicated or equivalent.


9. CONSISTENCY

Valid combinations include:

  - fixed + top
  - fixed + bottom
  - fixed + side
  - fixed + front_side
  - fixed + rear_side
  - fixed + left_side
  - fixed + right_side
  - robot_mounted + wrist
  - robot_mounted + left_wrist
  - robot_mounted + right_wrist
  - unknown + unknown

A FIXED camera MUST NOT be labeled wrist.

A ROBOT_MOUNTED camera MUST NOT be labeled top, bottom, or side.

{combo_rule}

Do not invent labels outside the vocabulary.
