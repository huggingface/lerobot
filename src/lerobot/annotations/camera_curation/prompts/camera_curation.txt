You are inspecting frames from ONE camera stream of a robot-manipulation dataset.
All frames come from the SAME camera during a single episode and are shown in
chronological order. Use ALL frames together. Do not classify from a single frame.

Perform THREE tasks:

1. determine whether the camera is usable;
2. determine whether its mount type is FIXED or ROBOT_MOUNTED;
3. classify its physical viewpoint.

Return one JSON object.

{task_context}1. QUALITY

Decide whether this camera view is usable for training a robot policy.

A USABLE view must show a genuine camera view of the robot manipulation scene
clearly enough to learn from.

Mark it UNUSABLE if the frames do not clearly show the real scene because of:

  - blur so EXTREME that the frame is unintelligible — you genuinely cannot tell
    what is in it (not merely soft focus or motion blur);
  - severe over- or under-exposure;
  - corrupted frames;
  - a static/frozen camera feed;
  - a placeholder, "no camera", or "no signal" image;
  - a test pattern;
  - an application/logo graphic;
  - a blank, solid-colour, or nearly-empty image that does not depict the task;
  - a TACTILE / touch-sensor readout rather than a camera view of the scene — e.g.
    a GelSight/DIGIT-style contact image, a uniform elastomer/gel pad, or a
    force/pressure heatmap (reason: "tactile sensor, not a camera view");
  - a DEPTH map / disparity image rather than a normal RGB photo — a smooth
    grayscale or false-colour distance map of the scene (reason: "depth map, not
    an RGB camera view");
  - a foreign obstruction blocking the lens such that no useful part of the task
    or robot is visible in any provided frame.

A real close-up is NOT an obstruction. If the work surface, manipulated object,
robot arm, gripper, or end effector fills most of the image, it is still a
genuine camera view and may be usable.

The robot's own arm, gripper, or end effector may partially block the workspace.
This is expected and MUST NOT make the camera unusable.

Some blur is NORMAL and stays USABLE. Wrist cameras especially are often a bit
blurry from motion. Mild or moderate blur, soft focus, or occasional motion blur
is FINE as long as you can still make out the scene, the robot, and the objects.
Do not flag a view just because it is not perfectly sharp — only when the blur is
so extreme the frame is unintelligible.

HUMANS. These are teleoperated recordings. Mark the camera UNUSABLE only when a
human is PROMINENTLY or SUBSTANTIALLY present as an operator/bystander (not part
of the task):

  - a person occupies a large part of the frame, or reaches into / leans over the
    working area;
  - a human hand or arm is prominently or persistently in or over the workspace;
  - a human is HOLDING A SECOND ARM / teleoperation "leader" device. The leader
    arm often looks IDENTICAL to the robot, but a human hand gripping an arm means
    the operator is in frame -> UNUSABLE. (The robot's own arm is mounted to the
    table/base, not held by a hand.)

A human who is only PARTIALLY visible or in the BACKGROUND is FINE and stays
USABLE: someone seen far off, small, cut off at the frame edge, or in the
background of the room, and not interacting with the robot or workspace, does NOT
make the view unusable. "A human is visible in the background" is by itself NEVER
a reason to flag — flag only a prominent operator in/over the working area or one
holding a leader arm.

EXCEPTION — the task itself involves a person. Some tasks are human-interactive
(e.g. a handshake, or a person handing/holding an object for the robot to
manipulate). Use the TASK CONTEXT above: if the stated task inherently involves a
person, OR the human is clearly a PARTICIPANT in the manipulation (interacting
with the robot/objects, not operating it), that is expected and the view stays
USABLE — do not flag the human in that case.

Carefully distinguish the robot's own (table/base-mounted) manipulator from BOTH a
human limb AND a human-HELD leader arm — a hand or wrist gripping the arm marks it
as operator-held, not the robot.

Set "blur_reason" to a short reason for an unusable verdict, otherwise null.

2. MOUNT TYPE

Choose exactly one:

  - "fixed"
  - "robot_mounted"
  - "unknown"

Determine mount type primarily from the behavior of the STATIC ENVIRONMENT across
time, NOT from the appearance or motion of the robot.

Identify static landmarks first

Look for scene elements that should remain stationary during the episode:

  - table edges;
  - work surfaces;
  - walls;
  - shelves;
  - fixtures;
  - trays;
  - stationary containers;
  - furniture;
  - background objects;
  - structural edges and corners.

Ignore the robot, gripper, manipulated objects, and humans when deciding whether
the CAMERA itself moves.

FIXED

Use "fixed" when static scene landmarks remain in approximately the same image
locations and preserve approximately the same perspective across frames.

For a fixed camera:

  - the static environment remains geometrically stable;
  - the table/background stays registered to the image;
  - the robot moves THROUGH the image;
  - the gripper may move substantially;
  - manipulated objects may move;
  - the arm may occupy a large fraction of the image;
  - the camera viewpoint itself does not travel with the robot.

A camera is still FIXED when the robot arm or gripper is very close to it.

A prominent gripper by itself is not decisive — see the wrist-vs-fixed
discriminator under ROBOT_MOUNTED below.

ROBOT_MOUNTED

Use "robot_mounted" ONLY when there is POSITIVE visual evidence that the camera
itself moves with the robot arm, wrist, gripper, or end effector.

The STATIC ENVIRONMENT must show camera-induced motion.

Strong evidence includes:

  - static table/background landmarks change position substantially across frames;
  - the perspective of the static environment changes;
  - static landmarks show global viewpoint or parallax changes;
  - those changes correlate with robot motion;
  - the workspace appears to move around the camera;
  - a visible part of the wrist/gripper may remain relatively stable in camera
    coordinates while the static environment moves.

ROBOT PART ANCHORED IN THE FOREGROUND (a strong wrist signal on its own): if a
piece of the ROBOT ITSELF sits large and close in the near foreground and stays
ANCHORED at roughly the same place in the frame across the frames — as if bolted
just in front of the lens — it is very likely a wrist camera, even before you
check background drift. This anchored part does NOT have to be a recognizable
"gripper": it can be the gripper jaws or fingers, the end-effector, the wrist
housing, or a smooth ARM LINK, and it is often
  - oversized and only partly in frame (cut off at the BOTTOM or a side edge),
  - out of focus / motion-blurred because it is inches from the lens,
  - a dark, plain, or featureless blob rather than an obvious mechanism.
Whenever such a robot part stays put in the frame while the WORKSPACE/BACKGROUND
shifts behind it, the camera is riding on the arm -> wrist. A wrist camera's
defining look is "a bit of the robot fixed in view, watching the world go by." So
do not require dramatic background motion, and do not require identifying an
actual gripper, to call wrist when a robot part sits anchored in the foreground.

COMMONLY MISSED WRIST: a wrist camera often looks like a CLOSE-UP, roughly
top-down view of the workspace with the gripper prominent, and is easily mistaken
for a FIXED "top" or "side". The giveaway is DRIFT of things that cannot move on
their own: a STATIONARY object (a cup, a bin, a sheet of paper, tape marks, the
table edge) that TRANSLATES across the frame or GROWS/SHRINKS in scale from one
frame to the next. Fixed objects do not move by themselves, so if a stationary
item slides across the image or changes size/perspective, the CAMERA moved ->
robot_mounted (wrist). Concretely: pick one stationary object and compare its
image position/size in the FIRST vs the LAST frame — a clear shift means wrist,
even if the view looks overhead and the background is a plain uniform surface.

WHOLE-SCENE RE-FRAMING = WRIST: when the ENTIRE composition swings between frames
— the same table, box, or container appears at a clearly DIFFERENT position,
scale, or angle each frame, so the shot looks re-aimed every time — the camera is
moving with the arm -> wrist. A FIXED camera keeps the scene composition CONSTANT
(the same background stays put in the same place) and only the arm/objects move
through it.

BEWARE A BLANK BACKDROP: a large uniform background — a GREEN SCREEN, a plain
wall, a seamless tabletop — has no trackable texture, so its drift is invisible
and the scene can look "static" even while the camera swings. Do NOT infer fixed
from an unchanging blank backdrop. Track the CONCRETE objects (the box, a bottle,
the container rim) instead; if THOSE jump around the frame or change scale, the
camera moved -> wrist.

Movement of the ROBOT alone is NOT evidence of camera movement.

Movement of manipulated OBJECTS alone is NOT evidence of camera movement (track a
STATIONARY object instead, per the drift check above).

Large differences between frames alone are NOT evidence of camera movement.

WRIST vs FIXED when a robot part (gripper, end-effector, or arm link) is in view —
the discriminator is whether that part is ANCHORED to the camera or TRAVELS
through the scene:

  - WRIST: the robot part sits in the near foreground and stays roughly ANCHORED
    at the same place/orientation in the frame while the background/scene shifts —
    the camera moves WITH the arm.
  - FIXED: the gripper/arm (however large) MOVES THROUGH the frame — it enters,
    crosses, and exits a stable scene whose background stays put; e.g. an overhead
    or side camera watching the arm pass below it.

So do NOT call it robot_mounted merely because a gripper is visible, or the arm
partially blocks an otherwise STABLE scene, or the gripper travels across a fixed
background — that is a fixed camera. But DO lean wrist when a robot part is close,
in the foreground, and anchored to the camera as above.

Required mount decision

Follow this order:

1. Identify static environment landmarks.
2. Compare their image position and perspective across ALL frames.
3. If they remain geometrically stable -> "fixed".
4. If they undergo clear GLOBAL viewpoint/parallax changes correlated with robot
   motion -> "robot_mounted".
5. If there is insufficient evidence -> "unknown".

"robot_mounted" requires POSITIVE evidence of CAMERA motion.

Do not infer robot mounting merely because you cannot prove that the camera is
fixed.

If stable background structure is visible and remains registered across frames,
the camera MUST be "fixed".

3. VIEW LABEL

Choose a label using ONLY this closed vocabulary:

  {vocabulary}

Classify hierarchically from mount_type.

If mount_type == "robot_mounted"

Use "wrist".

Default to plain "wrist".

DETERMINE HANDEDNESS FROM THE HAND SHAPE. If the camera shows a multi-fingered /
anthropomorphic hand (e.g. a humanoid or five-finger hand), you can usually tell
which hand it is — a LEFT hand and a RIGHT hand are MIRROR IMAGES, so the THUMB
sits on the opposite side of the fingers. Actively work it out:

  1. decide whether you are seeing the PALM or the BACK of the hand;
  2. locate the THUMB relative to the other four fingers;
  3. exactly as with a human hand, that fixes left vs right — assign "left_wrist"
     or "right_wrist".

Do this whenever the fingers and thumb are reasonably visible; do NOT default to
plain "wrist" just because it is easier. Fall back to plain "wrist" only when the
hand is a simple 2-jaw parallel gripper (no distinguishable thumb) or the hand's
structure genuinely cannot be read.

Do NOT infer handedness from SUPERFICIAL cues instead — use the hand's OWN
anatomy, never:

  - which side of the image the arm appears on;
  - the direction the gripper happens to point;
  - apparent image direction / mirroring / camera rotation.

If mount_type == "fixed"

Determine the camera's PHYSICAL VIEWING GEOMETRY relative to the manipulation
workspace.

Camera images may be tilted, rolled, rotated, or mirrored. Reason about the
physical camera pose, workspace plane, and robot coordinate frame. Do NOT map
image-left/image-right directly to robot-left/robot-right.

top

Use "top" for a genuinely OVERHEAD or NEAR-OVERHEAD camera.

The camera should be physically above the manipulation area and look mainly
DOWN ONTO the workspace. This is a canonical overhead role, but it does NOT need
to be perfect nadir / bird's-eye.

Positive evidence for "top":

  - the camera is physically above / over the workspace;
  - the manipulation area is primarily seen from above;
  - the work surface is the dominant scene plane;
  - object TOP faces are prominent;
  - the camera optical axis points mainly downward toward the manipulation region;
  - the overall scene reads as overhead or near-overhead, even if some perspective
    remains.

IMPORTANT: the following do NOT disqualify a true top camera by themselves:

  - moderate perspective;
  - some visible object side faces;
  - some visible robot vertical structure;
  - a small amount of wall/background;
  - a camera that is slightly offset from the exact workspace center.

Use "side" instead when the camera is clearly positioned around the workspace
perimeter and views it diagonally / obliquely from the side.

Practical rule:

  - physically over workspace + mainly downward -> top;
  - physically beside/perimeter + diagonal across workspace -> side.

When uncertain, do NOT decide from a single cue. Combine camera position,
viewing direction, dominant planes, and perspective.

bottom

Use "bottom" when the camera is physically below the manipulation workspace and
looks upward toward it.

side

Use a side label for ANY camera physically positioned around / beside the
workspace rather than directly overhead. This includes elevated cameras looking
strongly downward from the workspace perimeter.

A side camera may be elevated, tilted downward, and show substantial tabletop.
The deciding factor is the physical camera role: a camera positioned around or
beside the workspace is a side camera even when it is elevated and strongly
tilted downward.

Evidence supporting "side":

  - the camera looks primarily across the manipulation region;
  - strong near-to-far depth across the workspace;
  - strong perspective convergence;
  - substantial side/vertical surfaces;
  - the workspace is seen obliquely rather than mainly from above.

These are supporting cues, not independent hard rules.

TOP VS SIDE — REQUIRED GEOMETRIC TEST

For every fixed camera that could plausibly be either top or side, explicitly
compare:

  A. genuinely OVERHEAD / near-nadir -> "top"
  B. any clearly OBLIQUE / diagonal / perimeter view -> side

Use physical camera position relative to the workspace as the PRIMARY criterion,
and viewing direction as supporting evidence.

Do NOT use any single secondary cue as a veto. In particular:

  - a visible wall does NOT automatically mean side;
  - a visible robot column does NOT automatically mean side;
  - visible vertical faces do NOT automatically mean side;
  - a large tabletop does NOT automatically mean top;
  - an elevated camera does NOT automatically mean top.

When the geometry is genuinely borderline, ask whether the camera is physically
OVER the workspace or around its PERIMETER. If that cannot be established,
prefer "side". Reserve "top" for a clearly genuine overhead camera role.

SIDE DIRECTION

After deciding that the view is a side view, determine whether its physical
position relative to the ROBOT'S OWN coordinate frame is known.

The labels mean CAMERA POSITION:

  - front_side: camera is physically in FRONT of the robot;
  - rear_side: camera is physically BEHIND the robot;
  - left_side: camera is physically on the ROBOT'S LEFT;
  - right_side: camera is physically on the ROBOT'S RIGHT.

First establish the robot's physical FRONT/BACK orientation from the scene. The
front is the robot's working direction: where it normally reaches and where the
active manipulation workspace lies. The back may be supported by motor backs,
mounting structure, and cabling.

Useful FRONT/REAR evidence:

  - manipulated objects between the robot base and camera, with the arm reaching
    toward the camera -> supports front_side;
  - workspace beyond the robot, with the arm reaching away from the camera ->
    supports rear_side;
  - clear motor backs / mounting structure / cabling together with the arm
    reaching away -> supports rear_side.

Object/workspace geometry is generally stronger evidence than a few visible
motors or cables, because some robots expose those from many angles.

LEFT vs RIGHT — PHYSICAL CAMERA POSITION, NOT IMAGE DIRECTION

Determine whether the CAMERA itself is physically on the robot's left or right
side after establishing the robot's coordinate frame.

Do NOT use a rule such as:

  - robot front points toward image-left -> left_side;
  - robot front points toward image-right -> right_side.

Do NOT infer left/right from:

  - which half of the image contains the robot;
  - which image edge the arm enters from;
  - apparent image-left/image-right;
  - camera roll;
  - an assumed image mirroring convention.

Those image-space relationships can flip under camera rotation, mirroring, and
different camera poses.

Assign left_side or right_side ONLY when visible 3D scene geometry or a known
asymmetric robot feature lets you establish the robot's physical left/right
orientation and therefore the camera's physical side.

REQUIRED FOR A DIRECTIONAL SIDE LABEL

Use front_side / rear_side / left_side / right_side only when there is positive
visual evidence for that physical camera position.

If the robot's base/body is out of frame, or the robot coordinate frame cannot
be established reliably, use plain "side".


ROBOT-LAYOUT HEURISTIC FOR LEFT / RIGHT SIDE

When the camera is already known to be a SIDE view, use the robot's visible
layout and reaching direction to infer which physical side the camera is on.

A strong practical cue is:

  - robot body / base appears toward the RIGHT side of the image and the arm
    reaches LEFTWARD across the workspace -> camera is usually on the ROBOT'S
    RIGHT side -> "right_side";

  - robot body / base appears toward the LEFT side of the image and the arm
    reaches RIGHTWARD across the workspace -> camera is usually on the ROBOT'S
    LEFT side -> "left_side".

Think of this as viewing the robot from one side across its workspace: the robot
body tends to occupy the near-side image edge while the arm reaches away across
the scene.

Use this cue from the ROBOT GEOMETRY, not from image-left/image-right alone.
The important pattern is the combination of:

  1. where the robot body/base is located in the image, and
  2. the direction in which the arm extends / reaches into the workspace.

If the two cues form the patterns above, prefer the corresponding directional
side label instead of plain "side".

Examples:

  robot on image RIGHT + reaching LEFT  -> right_side
  robot on image LEFT  + reaching RIGHT -> left_side

Do not invert this rule just because the camera image may be rolled or tilted.
If the image is known or strongly suspected to be horizontally mirrored, avoid
using this heuristic unless other robot geometry confirms the same conclusion.


DIRECTIONAL SIDE LABELS — PREFER THEM WHEN THE ROBOT LAYOUT SUPPORTS THEM

Once a fixed camera is classified as a SIDE view, do NOT stop at plain "side"
when the robot layout gives a clear left/right pattern.

Use these patterns as actionable evidence:

  - robot body/base on image RIGHT + arm reaching LEFT
      -> output "right_side"

  - robot body/base on image LEFT + arm reaching RIGHT
      -> output "left_side"

These are not merely weak candidates. When the body/base position and reach
direction are both clear and consistent, choose the directional label directly.

Use plain "side" only when at least one of the following is true:

  - the robot body/base position is unclear;
  - the reaching direction is unclear;
  - the image is likely horizontally mirrored;
  - the visible geometry contradicts the heuristic;
  - the camera is lateral but the robot layout cannot establish a direction.

Do NOT keep "side" just because full 3D reconstruction of the robot coordinate
frame is unavailable. The body-position + reach-direction pattern is sufficient
when it is visually clear.


Plain "side" is a VALID, CONFIDENT classification when the camera is clearly
lateral/oblique but its physical direction cannot be established. It is not a
failure and must be preferred over an unsupported directional guess.

CANDIDATE BEHAVIOR FOR DIRECTIONAL UNCERTAINTY

If the camera is clearly a side view but a direction is only plausible rather
than established:

  - set view_label to "side";
  - put the plausible directional label(s) in "candidates" with lower confidence;
  - do NOT promote a weak left/right/front/rear guess to view_label.

If mount_type == "unknown"

Use "unknown" unless the physical view category can nevertheless be established
with strong evidence.

CONSISTENCY

Valid combinations include:

  - fixed + top
  - fixed + bottom
  - fixed + side
  - fixed + front_side
  - fixed + rear_side
  - fixed + left_side
  - fixed + right_side
  - robot_mounted + wrist
  - robot_mounted + left_wrist
  - robot_mounted + right_wrist
  - unknown + unknown

A FIXED camera MUST NOT be labeled wrist.

A ROBOT_MOUNTED camera MUST NOT be labeled top, bottom, or side.

{combo_rule}

Do not invent labels outside the vocabulary.

OUTPUT
